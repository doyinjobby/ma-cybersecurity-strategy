# M&A Supplier Criticality & Risk-Tiering Model

**Author:** Dr. Olajumoke Bello, PhD  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Classification:** Public Portfolio Sample  
**Status:** Illustrative M&A Third-Party Risk Deliverable

---

## 1. Purpose

This model provides a structured approach for assessing the criticality and cybersecurity risk of third-party suppliers identified during merger and acquisition (M&A) due diligence.

The objective is to determine the level of supplier assurance required based on the supplier's access to information, systems and business processes, together with the potential operational, regulatory, financial and cybersecurity impact of supplier failure or compromise.

Risk tiering enables assurance activities to be proportionate to risk rather than applying the same level of assessment to every supplier.

---

## 2. Supplier Discovery

The first stage is to establish an inventory of relevant suppliers and third-party dependencies.

Information collected should include:

- Supplier name and service provided
- Business owner
- Contract owner
- Service criticality
- Systems or applications supported
- Data accessed, processed or stored
- Privileged or administrative access
- Network connectivity
- Cloud or hosting dependencies
- Subcontractors and fourth parties
- Geographic location and data residency
- Contract expiry and renewal dates
- Existing security assurance evidence
- Business continuity dependencies

The supplier inventory provides the foundation for criticality assessment and subsequent cybersecurity due diligence.

---

## 3. Criticality Assessment Criteria

Supplier criticality should be assessed using factors such as:

### Business Dependency

Consider whether failure of the supplier could materially disrupt a critical business service or acquisition integration activity.

### Information Sensitivity

Determine whether the supplier accesses, processes, stores or transmits confidential, commercially sensitive or personal information.

### System Access

Assess whether the supplier has logical, physical, remote, privileged or administrative access to organisational systems.

### Regulatory and Privacy Exposure

Consider whether the relationship creates obligations under applicable regulatory, contractual or data-protection requirements.

### Operational Resilience

Assess the organisation's dependency on the supplier and whether alternative providers or recovery arrangements are available.

### Concentration Risk

Determine whether multiple critical services depend on the same supplier, technology platform, geographic region or underlying service provider.

### Fourth-Party Dependency

Identify material subcontractors, cloud providers or other fourth parties upon which the supplier relies.

## 4. Supplier Risk Tiers

Suppliers may be classified into four indicative risk tiers.

### Tier 1 — Critical

A supplier may be classified as Critical where compromise, prolonged outage or failure could cause significant business, regulatory, financial or cybersecurity impact.

Typical characteristics include:

- Supports critical business services
- Processes highly sensitive or regulated information
- Holds privileged or administrative access
- Provides significant cloud, infrastructure or managed services
- Creates material operational resilience dependency
- Has limited viable substitutes
- Failure could materially affect the acquisition or integration programme

**Assurance Level:** Enhanced due diligence and ongoing monitoring.

---

### Tier 2 — High

A High-risk supplier may have significant access to organisational information or systems but may not represent an immediate threat to business continuity if unavailable.

Typical characteristics include:

- Processes sensitive information
- Has system or network access
- Supports important business processes
- Creates meaningful privacy or regulatory exposure
- May rely on significant fourth parties

**Assurance Level:** Comprehensive security assessment and periodic reassessment.

---

### Tier 3 — Medium

A Medium-risk supplier generally has limited access to sensitive information or important systems and can be replaced without significant disruption.

Typical characteristics include:

- Limited system access
- Processes low-to-moderate sensitivity information
- Supports non-critical services
- Has manageable operational dependency

**Assurance Level:** Proportionate security assessment and evidence review.

---

### Tier 4 — Low

A Low-risk supplier has minimal cybersecurity or operational impact.

Typical characteristics include:

- No access to sensitive information
- No privileged or network access
- Does not support critical business services
- Minimal regulatory or resilience dependency

**Assurance Level:** Basic due diligence and contractual requirements.

---

## 5. Assurance Requirements by Tier

### Tier 1 — Critical

Examples of assurance evidence may include:

- ISO 27001 certification and scope
- SOC 2 Type II report where applicable
- Penetration testing summary
- Vulnerability management evidence
- Incident response arrangements
- Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery evidence
- Data protection assessment
- Cybersecurity insurance where appropriate
- Subprocessor or fourth-party information
- Contractual security obligations
- Remediation plan for identified control gaps

### Tier 2 — High

Assurance may include:

- Security questionnaire
- ISO 27001 or equivalent assurance evidence
- SOC 2 report where applicable
- Data protection assessment
- Vulnerability management evidence
- Incident management arrangements
- Business continuity evidence
- Contract security review

### Tier 3 — Medium

Assurance may include:

- Proportionate security questionnaire
- Relevant security policies
- Data protection review where applicable
- Basic continuity assessment
- Contractual security requirements

### Tier 4 — Low

Assurance may include:

- Basic supplier due diligence
- Confirmation of information and system access
- Standard contractual security requirements
- Periodic reassessment where appropriate

## 6. M&A Deal-Impact Considerations

Supplier findings should not be assessed solely as standalone third-party risks.

During an acquisition, findings should also be considered in terms of their potential impact on the transaction and integration programme.

Possible classifications include:

- **Deal Breaker** — supplier dependency creates unacceptable exposure
- **Pre-Close Condition** — action or contractual protection required before completion
- **Day-1 Priority** — immediate post-acquisition action required
- **30–90 Day Remediation** — risk can be managed during integration
- **Longer-Term Improvement** — risk can be incorporated into ongoing supplier governance

This creates a direct link between supplier assurance and M&A decision-making.

---

## 7. Supplier Risk Treatment

Identified supplier risks may be treated through:

- **Mitigate** — strengthen controls or require supplier remediation
- **Avoid** — discontinue or replace the supplier relationship
- **Transfer** — allocate elements of risk contractually or through appropriate insurance
- **Accept** — formally accept residual risk within approved risk appetite

Material risks should have an accountable owner, agreed treatment action, target completion date and evidence of remediation.

---

## 8. Post-Acquisition Supplier Integration

Following acquisition, supplier relationships should be incorporated into the acquiring organisation's third-party risk management framework.

Priority activities may include:

- Consolidating supplier inventories
- Revalidating criticality classifications
- Identifying duplicate suppliers
- Reviewing overlapping technology services
- Reassessing Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers
- Aligning security contractual requirements
- Reviewing data-processing arrangements
- Identifying concentration and fourth-party risks
- Tracking remediation actions
- Establishing reassessment schedules
- Integrating suppliers into ongoing monitoring

---

## 9. Governance and Escalation

Critical supplier risks should be reported through the appropriate M&A and cybersecurity governance structure.

Escalation may involve:

- Information Security
- Procurement
- Legal
- Privacy
- Business Continuity
- Service Owners
- CISO
- CIO
- M&A Steering Committee

The level of escalation should reflect supplier criticality, residual risk and potential impact on the transaction or combined organisation.

---

## 10. Portfolio Outcome

This model demonstrates how supplier assurance can be prioritised according to business dependency and cybersecurity risk.

The approach provides the following traceability:

**Supplier Discovery → Criticality Assessment → Risk Tiering → Due Diligence → Assurance Evidence → Risk Treatment → M&A Deal Impact → Post-Acquisition Monitoring**

This enables third-party cybersecurity risk to be managed proportionately throughout the M&A lifecycle.
